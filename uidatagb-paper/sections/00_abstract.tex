\begin{abstract}
Deep-learning studies on gallbladder ultrasound usually give accuracy and a handful of heatmaps as proof of interpretability, and rarely test whether either survives closer scrutiny. This paper does that scrutiny directly on a ResNet-50 trained for nine-class gallbladder ultrasound classification on the public UIdataGB dataset, and the scrutiny itself changes the headline number. An initial split, de-duplicated only by single-orientation image hashing, gave 93.05\% accuracy; a closer audit found near-identical frames from the same imaging session, and rotation-augmented duplicates the dataset's own release pipeline introduces, still crossing the train/validation boundary. Correcting both and holding out a genuine test set drops mean accuracy across four independently seeded models to 72.56\%~$\pm$~2.35\%, which we report as the real result rather than the inflated one. Five attribution methods (Grad-CAM, Grad-CAM++, Saliency Maps, Eigen-CAM, Score-CAM) are compared for consistency, faithfulness, stability, a parameter-randomization sanity test, a shortcut audit, and calibration. Grad-CAM++ and Score-CAM are the most reproducible across seeds; on faithfulness all three CAM methods perform similarly, and no single method wins on every metric. A null-control test---cosine similarity between untrained models and between synthetic random heatmaps, which the consistency metric had never been checked against---confirms the corrected consistency score clears the untrained-model and ReLU-Gaussian-synthetic floors by roughly 2.5$\times$; a third, uniform-random-synthetic floor sits much higher (0.755) and is cleared by only $\sim$1.1$\times$, showing the choice of null distribution itself matters rather than all floors giving the same margin. A separate, targeted audit finds a real batch-correlated shortcut: a classifier trained only to identify which acquisition batch a Carcinoma image came from reaches 82.88\% accuracy (8.3$\times$ chance) even after correcting for cluster-level leakage, and a linear probe on the disease classifier's own features recovers batch identity at 74.75\% (4.5$\times$ chance) for that class, directly implicating the classifier these explanations describe. Extending that probe to all nine disease classes finds every one reaching 100\% batch-identification accuracy, though this uses a per-batch split that likely still carries some of the same near-duplicate-frame leakage the diagnostic's own cluster-aware re-verification partly corrects for, so the qualitative conclusion (the signature is not Carcinoma-specific) is more secure than the exact 100\% figure. A noise-robustness sweep adds another caution: accuracy on the corrected model falls from 90\% to near chance under still-subtle Gaussian noise. The explanations are measurably faithful to the classifier's behaviour; that is not the same as proof its decisions are clinically reliable, and the shortcut and noise findings are concrete reasons for caution beyond the usual internal-validation caveats. This remains an internal-validation study: it does not establish multicentre generalizability, patient-level validation, or clinician-confirmed localization. Several methodological gaps raised during external review are addressed directly: bootstrap intervals now accompany every XAI table, direct EXIF timestamp evidence supports the acquisition-session inference used throughout, and the full battery, five methods for consistency, three for faithfulness (by design, not omission: Eigen-CAM is class-agnostic and Saliency attributes in a different space, as detailed in Methods), and Grad-CAM++ for stability, sanity, and the shortcut audit, has been re-run on the corrected split, confirming the same method ranking holds even though the underlying classifier's accuracy does not. What remains open---principally clinician-confirmed localization---is reported honestly rather than elided.
\end{abstract}
